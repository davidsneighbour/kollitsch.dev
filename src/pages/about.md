I am Patrick Kollitsch. I build websites, solve technical problems, work with SEO, and have been tinkering with the web for long enough to remember when having a personal website was simply what you did.

I have been on the internet since 1994. Last century, in other words.

Since then, browsers, search engines, programming languages, publishing systems, hosting platforms, and approximately seventeen thousand JavaScript frameworks have come and gone. I am still here, building things, taking things apart, figuring out why they broke, and writing down what I learned along the way.

## What is this?

**kollitsch.dev** is my personal corner of the web.

It is part notebook, part technical blog, part reference library, and occasionally a place to document something mostly because I know I will have forgotten it six months from now.

Most of what you will find here revolves around websites and the systems surrounding them: development, SEO, performance, accessibility, hosting, security, tooling, automation, and the peculiar problems that appear once theory meets an actual website.

I prefer practical solutions over fashionable ones. If three lines of CSS solve a problem, I do not particularly want a framework for it. If a framework genuinely makes the problem easier, I am perfectly happy to use one.

The point is not to use the most impressive technology. The point is to make things work well.

## How did we get here?

I first got online in **1994**, when the web was still small enough to feel like somewhere you could wander around.

Personal websites were common. People made pages because they had something to say, something to show, or simply because making a website was interesting. Long before social networks concentrated everything onto a handful of platforms, your website was your place on the internet.

I rather liked that idea.

Over the years, tinkering with websites turned into building them professionally. That led to development, content management systems, servers, analytics, search engines, SEO, automation, and all the other disciplines that accumulate when you spend enough time asking, "How does this actually work?"

The tools have changed considerably since 1994. My approach has not changed quite as much: understand the problem, find the simplest sensible solution, build it properly, and keep learning.

This site is part of that process.

## What can I do for you?

I build and improve websites.

Sometimes that means creating something new. Sometimes it means taking an existing site that is slow, difficult to maintain, invisible to search engines, technically outdated, or simply not doing its job particularly well and figuring out what needs to change.

My work sits somewhere between **web development, technical problem-solving, and SEO**.

That can include site architecture, performance, technical SEO, development, migrations, automation, analytics, hosting, security, and the less glamorous debugging work required to make all of those things cooperate.

I am particularly interested in the intersection between development and SEO because the two are rarely as separate as organisations like to pretend. A good website needs both sound engineering and a clear understanding of how people and search engines actually use it.

If you have a website that needs building, fixing, untangling, or improving, that is probably where I can help.

## Why should you come back?

Because I keep notes.

A lot of them eventually become articles here.

You will find practical explanations, tutorials, snippets, experiments, and solutions to problems I encountered while working on real websites. Some are substantial guides. Others answer the kind of oddly specific question that sends you through twelve search results before you finally find the one command you needed.

I write primarily for people who build, run, maintain, or otherwise have to deal with websites.

There will be development. There will be SEO. There will be tools, experiments, mistakes, fixes, and probably an unreasonable amount of attention paid to small details.

In other words, much like the web itself, **kollitsch.dev is never really finished**.

Which seems like a reasonably good reason to come back.
